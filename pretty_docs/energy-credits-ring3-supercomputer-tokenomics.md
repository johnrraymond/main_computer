# Main Computer ENERGY CREDITS: Tokenomics and the Computational Economy

By John R. Raymond

## 1. The Economic Thesis

Main Computer is building a distributed supercomputer that we do not have to own.

Instead of purchasing and maintaining all the hardware necessary to operate an increasingly powerful computational system, we purchase computational work from independent providers. Those providers supply processing capacity, receive ENERGY CREDITS in compensation, and collectively form the Ring 3 Supercomputer.

The greater the participating computational capacity, the greater the potential of Ring 3.

ENERGY CREDITS are the currency that makes this arrangement economically possible.

But Main Computer is not simply a buyer of computation. We also sell computational services, operate a restricted marketplace in which users can sell services to one another, and maintain the trusted infrastructure necessary to govern the system.

This produces four distinct functions:

| Ring   | Function                  | Economic relationship                                                      |
| ------ | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ring 0 | Trust and governance      | Exclusive authority over authorization, verification, and enforcement      |
| Ring 1 | Commercial services       | We sell secure computation and contracted computational services           |
| Ring 2 | Peer-to-peer commerce     | Users buy and sell services under Library auditing agreements              |
| Ring 3 | Distributed supercomputer | We are the exclusive purchaser of computation supplied by external workers |

The rings are not four different currencies. They are four different operating environments within one computational economy.

ENERGY CREDITS move through that economy as participants purchase, perform, and sell useful work.

## 2. Ring 3: Building a Supercomputer Through Economic Participation

Ring 3 is the foundation of the system's distributed computational capacity.

Independent owners contribute computers, processors, GPUs, AI models, and other eligible computational resources. In exchange for successfully performed work, they receive ENERGY CREDITS.

We are the exclusive purchaser of Ring 3 computation.

That exclusivity establishes a single purchasing and coordination authority for the external computational network. Individual customers cannot directly enter Ring 3 and independently purchase work. They obtain access through services we sell in Ring 1.

We use Ring 3 for our own computational requirements, directing suitable workloads away from our protected infrastructure and into the distributed network.

This has two economic effects.

First, we can purchase computational work from providers competing to supply capacity, potentially reducing our operating expenses.

Second, we preserve our own computers for workloads requiring the security, confidentiality, reliability, or execution guarantees of our protected infrastructure.

As Ring 3 expands, we can coordinate larger collections of resources. The network becomes capable of accepting increasingly extensive computational assignments, provided the software can divide, distribute, execute, and validate the work effectively.

The ultimate objective is not merely a marketplace containing millions of computers.

It is a distributed supercomputer capable of organizing those computers into a coordinated computational resource.

That requires scheduling, workload decomposition, data management, recovery from failures, result verification, and payment settlement. Not all computations benefit equally from geographically distributed execution, so the system must match workloads to the capabilities and communication constraints of the network.

The owners provide the computing capacity. Main Computer provides the coordination. ENERGY CREDITS compensate the owners for computational work.

## 3. Ring 1: Selling Computation to the World

Ring 1 is our commercial interface with customers.

We offer two principal categories of computational service.

### Secure computation

Customers can purchase computation performed on our protected infrastructure.

These services are intended for work that cannot appropriately be entrusted to the peer-to-peer marketplace.

A customer may need stronger confidentiality, controlled execution, restricted access, or additional security guarantees.

By purchasing inexpensive external computation for our own eligible workloads, we preserve more of our protected infrastructure for precisely these customers.

The commercial objective is to sell secure computational capacity at prices that reflect its capabilities, assurance requirements, and operating costs.

### Contracted supercomputer access

Ring 1 also allows customers to negotiate contracts for computational work executed on the Ring 3 Supercomputer.

The customer purchases a service from us, not directly from Ring 3.

We purchase the necessary Ring 3 computation, organize execution, enforce the applicable contract, and deliver the resulting service.

These agreements can specify workload requirements, service levels, acceptable execution environments, verification procedures, confidentiality limitations, and pricing.

The customer receives access to the computational capacity of the distributed network without becoming a direct Ring 3 purchaser.

This creates a second commercial opportunity: we can sell access to computing capacity that we do not physically own.

A Ring 1 contract fulfilled through Ring 3 does not automatically carry the same security guarantees as computation executed entirely on our protected machines. The contract defines what we provide, while Ring 0 enforces the relevant authorization and verification rules.

The distinction allows Ring 1 to offer both premium secure services and contracted distributed computation without confusing the two.

## 4. Ring 2: The Restricted Peer-to-Peer Economy

Ring 2 provides a separate marketplace in which users can buy and sell computational services directly to one another.

This marketplace is neither completely public nor completely anonymous.

Participants may conduct business under pseudonymous identities, limiting unnecessary public disclosure of their activities.

However, participation requires both parties to accept the Library's authority to audit their transactions.

A buyer and seller must consent to the applicable rules before their transaction proceeds.

The Library can investigate conduct, examine the relevant transaction records, and enforce the marketplace's terms. Established violations may result in restrictions, suspension, or permanent banning.

This creates a form of conditional commercial privacy.

Participants need not publicly disclose every transaction, but they cannot demand immunity from the marketplace's enforcement authority.

The system should preserve evidence supporting enforcement decisions, define prohibited conduct, and provide a procedure for disputing erroneous findings.

Economically, Ring 2 allows users to build businesses within the broader Main Computer ecosystem.

They may offer specialized models, data services, applications, computational processing, or other permitted products.

Buyers compensate sellers in ENERGY CREDITS.

If Main Computer establishes marketplace fees, those fees can provide an additional revenue source for maintaining the platform, settlement infrastructure, and auditing system. The fee schedule is a separate policy to be established.

Ring 2 therefore expands the economic uses of ENERGY CREDITS without requiring Main Computer itself to produce every service.

## 5. Ring 0: The Authority Behind the Economy

Ring 0 is not an ordinary commercial marketplace.

It is the trust and enforcement authority.

The other rings can perform computation, sell services, purchase work, and transfer ENERGY CREDITS, but none of those activities independently grants authority over the system.

Ring 0 governs who is authorized to purchase Ring 3 computation, which services may operate in Ring 1, and whether Ring 2 transactions satisfy the applicable participation rules.

It also establishes the procedures through which computational results, transaction records, and contractual obligations are verified or accepted.

A Ring 3 worker cannot become trusted merely because it has earned substantial ENERGY CREDITS.

A Ring 2 business cannot purchase immunity from the Library's auditing authority.

A Ring 1 customer cannot acquire control over Ring 0 simply by paying for a premium service.

The trust architecture remains separate from the monetary architecture.

We buy Ring 3 computation, sell Ring 1 services, permit restricted commerce in Ring 2, and trust only Ring 0.

## 6. How ENERGY CREDITS Circulate

The computational economy creates three principal commercial flows.

### Ring 3: We spend credits

We purchase computational work from external providers.

ENERGY CREDITS flow from Main Computer to the owners of computational resources.

In return, Main Computer receives computational work that can support its internal operations or contractual obligations to customers.

### Ring 1: We earn credits

Customers purchase secure computation or contracted access to distributed computation.

ENERGY CREDITS flow from customers to Main Computer.

Some of those earnings support our infrastructure, some pay for Ring 3 procurement, and the remainder may contribute to operating surplus after other expenses.

### Ring 2: Users exchange credits

Users purchase services from other users.

ENERGY CREDITS flow between participants as payment for their transactions.

Main Computer facilitates and governs the marketplace, potentially collecting defined service fees.

These flows reinforce one another.

Ring 1 customers create commercial demand. Ring 3 providers supply computational capacity. Ring 2 creates additional opportunities to earn and spend credits.

ENERGY CREDITS connect these activities in a single accounting and settlement system.

Crucially, the circulation of credits is not the same as the creation of credits.

A customer spending ten credits and a worker earning ten credits does not create twenty new credits. Existing purchasing power has changed hands.

The same credits can support successive transactions throughout the network.

## 7. The Economic Engine

The Ring 3 Supercomputer creates a potential reinforcing economic cycle.

More demand for computational services gives Main Computer a reason to purchase additional Ring 3 capacity.

Greater purchasing demand creates earning opportunities for computational providers.

Those opportunities may encourage additional providers to join the network, increasing the available hardware, models, and specialized capabilities.

Expanded capacity can make Ring 3 attractive for more workloads.

More useful workloads can support additional commercial demand through Ring 1.

ENERGY CREDITS facilitate each exchange.

The cycle is not automatic. Providers must find participation economically worthwhile, customers must receive valuable services, and Main Computer must coordinate work reliably enough to support both.

The economic objective is to create conditions under which supplying useful computation is profitable, purchasing it is worthwhile, and organizing the exchange generates sustainable revenue.

## 8. Where Main Computer Makes Money

Main Computer has three potential sources of operating income.

First, it sells secure computation using its own protected infrastructure.

Second, it sells contracted computation fulfilled through the Ring 3 Supercomputer. The commercial margin comes from selling a managed service for more than the total cost of procuring and delivering it.

Third, it may collect fees for operating the restricted Ring 2 marketplace.

The operating relationship can be expressed as:

Operating profit = Ring 1 secure-service revenue + Ring 1 contracted-service revenue + Ring 2 fee revenue − Ring 3 procurement costs − Infrastructure expenses − Verification, settlement, enforcement, and other operating costs.

Ring 2 fee revenue is included only if such fees are adopted.

This arrangement creates an important distinction between ownership of computational hardware and commercial control of a computational service.

We do not need to own every computer whose work we sell.

We need to be able to purchase, coordinate, verify, and deliver that work under commercially sustainable terms.

At the same time, retaining our own protected hardware allows us to offer security-sensitive services that cannot appropriately be outsourced.

The two businesses complement one another.

## 9. Supply, Issuance, and Monetary Value

ENERGY CREDITS are Main Computer's native blockchain currency, accounted for using eighteen decimal places.

The Hub also maintains private service-credit balances backed by native-value accounting, allowing computational purchases and worker earnings to be recorded without requiring a separate public blockchain transaction for every task.

The distinction between monetary supply and computational activity is essential.

There are four separate economic quantities:

**Total supply:** The amount of native currency that exists.

**Circulating liquidity:** The amount available for spending, excluding currency restricted by reserves, holds, or other constraints.

**Transactional demand:** The quantity of credits participants need to purchase computational services and meet settlement obligations.

**Computational throughput:** The volume of useful work the network performs and sells.

These quantities do not automatically increase together.

A growing supercomputer can perform more work without requiring the native currency supply to increase at the same rate.

Existing credits may circulate faster as the marketplace becomes more active.

Likewise, creating additional native currency does not itself add processors, GPUs, customers, or useful computational work.

Main Computer's existing genesis architecture provides a very large nominal allocation, much of it held in a governed reserve. The codebase also contains controlled native issuance mechanisms.

The reserve, initial supply, subsequent minting, and circulation must be addressed through an explicit monetary policy.

Reserve distributions release existing currency. Native minting increases the monetary supply. Payments for computational work transfer purchasing power between participants.

These are different operations and must be accounted for independently.

The economic value of ENERGY CREDITS cannot be guaranteed merely by naming them after energy or by creating an enormous reserve.

Their practical utility comes from what holders can purchase within the network and the conditions under which credits can be exchanged.

Their external market price, if traded, would also depend on liquidity, expectations, supply policy, and demand.

ENERGY CREDITS therefore provide the medium for the computational economy without requiring a fixed exchange rate to electricity or any particular unit of processing power.

## 10. The Incentive for Computational Providers

A Ring 3 provider participates because it can receive compensation for work its hardware performs.

The provider must evaluate the costs of electricity, hardware depreciation, software, maintenance, connectivity, and operational risk against the value of credits earned.

A provider with underutilized computational capacity may find it economical to sell that capacity when the offered compensation exceeds the incremental cost of performing the work.

Specialized providers may command higher prices when they offer scarce hardware, capable models, or superior performance.

Because Main Computer is the exclusive Ring 3 purchaser, its procurement system must attract and retain sufficient supply.

That requires prices and purchasing policies that providers consider commercially worthwhile.

The network's long-term capacity depends on voluntary participation remaining economically attractive.

Ring 3 therefore combines distributed hardware ownership with centralized purchasing authority and coordinated computational execution.

## 11. Why the Ring 3 Supercomputer Can Grow

Conventional supercomputing infrastructure requires substantial investment in physical hardware, facilities, power, networking, and maintenance.

Main Computer's Ring 3 approach distributes much of the hardware investment among independent participants.

Providers purchase and maintain their own eligible equipment.

Main Computer supplies the coordination and purchasing system that turns independently available capacity into organized computational services.

This can permit expansion without requiring Main Computer to finance every additional processor directly.

However, actual usable capacity is constrained by workload structure, communication latency, bandwidth, reliability, data availability, and verification costs.

Highly parallel tasks may exploit large numbers of distributed workers effectively. Tasks requiring extremely frequent communication or tightly synchronized shared memory may be better served by specialized local clusters.

The goal is therefore to build a supercomputer whose effective computational power increases as additional useful resources join the network, while preserving the ability to route unsuitable work elsewhere.

The economic architecture makes expansion possible. The computational architecture must make the resulting capacity useful.

## 12. The Strategic Outcome

Main Computer combines three businesses and one governing authority.

The first business is secure computation performed on infrastructure we control.

The second is the sale of access to a distributed supercomputer assembled from computational resources owned by other participants.

The third is a restricted peer-to-peer marketplace in which users establish their own commercial relationships using ENERGY CREDITS.

Ring 0 governs the trust boundaries of all three.

The resulting system is designed to reduce the capital required to expand computational capacity, preserve protected hardware for security-sensitive customers, and create economic incentives for independent providers to contribute resources.

It also provides an expanding range of uses for ENERGY CREDITS.

The credits compensate computational suppliers, enable customers to purchase services, facilitate transactions between users, and connect the network's commercial operations.

The strongest source of demand is not speculative trading in the currency. It is the continuing demand for useful computation and services priced in that currency.

## Conclusion: ENERGY CREDITS and the Ring 3 Supercomputer

The central proposition is straightforward.

We are building a supercomputer whose computational resources can be contributed by independent owners around the world.

We purchase those resources through Ring 3, using ENERGY CREDITS to compensate the providers.

We preserve our own protected computers for customers who need secure computation, while also selling contracted access to the Ring 3 Supercomputer through Ring 1.

We allow users to buy and sell services through Ring 2, under semi-anonymous arrangements that remain subject to Library auditing and enforcement.

And we retain Ring 0 as the authority governing trust.

The economic model does not require Main Computer to own all the hardware. It requires Main Computer to organize computational supply, establish commercially useful services, manage monetary settlement, and preserve the system's trust boundaries.

If successful, increasing demand supports increasing computational capacity. Increasing capacity permits additional useful services. Those services create further opportunities to earn and spend ENERGY CREDITS.

The objective is an expanding computational economy built around an expanding distributed supercomputer.

**ENERGY CREDITS are the currency. Ring 3 is the supercomputer. Ring 1 brings its power to customers. Ring 2 permits governed commerce among users. Ring 0 keeps the system trustworthy.**
